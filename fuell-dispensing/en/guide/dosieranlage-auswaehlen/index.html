Choosing a dispensing system: the practical guide

A dispensing system is not an off-the-shelf machine. Two paint manufacturers with a similar range can need completely different systems – because the number of components, container sizes, throughput and space differ. This guide shows which points you should clarify before an enquiry and how to derive the right type of system from them.

Why an automatic dispensing system pays off

Anyone who weighs out recipes by hand knows the weak points: every component has to go on the scale individually, containers are opened and decanted, and the documentation is written afterwards. Errors often only show up during re-tinting or in quality control.

An automatic dispensing system takes over the weighing according to the recipe, checks every quantity with a scale and stores every operation. The advantages users mention most often:

  • Reproducible batches: every batch matches the previous one, because quantities do not depend on how the day is going.
  • Small orders produced economically: even the smallest quantities can be made quickly and without set-up effort.
  • Fewer open containers: raw materials come through pipes from the tank farm instead of being decanted.
  • Complete documentation: the dispensing log shows what went into which batch and when.

Step 1: Record components and raw materials

The starting point is a list of all raw materials that are to be dispensed automatically. For each component, the following matter:

  • Viscosity and flow behaviour: solvents, binders, pastes and additives behave very differently (see viscosity).
  • Solids content: pigmented products tend to sedimentation and need recirculation or agitators.
  • Reactivity: UV-curing or reactive substances place their own demands on storage and valves.
  • Hazardous properties: flammable solvents require Ex protection.
  • Annual consumption: it decides whether a substance comes from a stationary tank or from an exchangeable container.

The number of components determines the size of the dispensing centre. Füll builds systems with just a few valves up to systems with 64 components and more.

Step 2: Define throughput and containers

The second question is: how much is dispensed per day – and into which containers? The smallest containers such as bottles need different systems than 200-litre drums or process vessels.

Step 3: Choose the measuring principle

Gravimetric systems measure the mass with a scale or a mass flow meter. This is the standard when high reproducibility and traceability are required. Volumetric systems dispense a defined volume, for example with a piston pump, and are particularly fast, for instance when tinting into pre-filled containers.

Both methods can be combined: Füll dispensing centres can be equipped with scales or with the volumetric dispensing unit HX5. You will find the detailed comparison in the guide Gravimetric or volumetric dispensing.

Step 4: Plan storage, pipes and agitation as well

A dispensing system is only as good as its supply of raw materials. Do the components come from stationary tanks, from containers in a rack or from exchangeable containers? Are they recirculated so that nothing settles? How do they get to the system?

That is why Füll plans tank farms, pipes, recirculation and agitators together with the dispensing system. With a correct process design of the storage, the effort for rework such as re-tinting drops considerably. More on this in the guide Planning a tank farm.

Step 5: Safety and regulations

Solvent-based paints, coatings and printing inks almost always mean potentially explosive areas. Systems, terminals and agitators must then be designed according to ATEX. Storage areas with liquids hazardous to water are subject to the requirements of German water law (WHG), for example an approved overfill protection.

Füll is certified according to ATEX for the manufacture of equipment and is a WHG specialist company. Details in the guide Ex protection for dispensing systems.

Step 6: Software and connection

The software manages orders, recipes, stocks and logs. At Füll this is CALIPSO, in use since 1993 and continuously developed further. Clarify in advance:

  • Should orders and recipes come from the ERP or MES system (ERP connection)?
  • Will colour measurement data be imported?
  • Which access rights do operators, the laboratory and production need?
  • How long must dispensing logs be kept for batch traceability?

Checklist for your enquiry

With this information, a manufacturer can draw up a reliable concept and quotation:

  • List of components with viscosity, Ex classification and annual consumption
  • Containers and batch sizes (smallest and largest quantity)
  • Number of orders per day and required accuracy
  • Existing storage, space, room height and Ex zones
  • Desired connection to ERP, MES or colour measurement
  • Budget and schedule

Frequently asked questions

How accurately does a dispensing system dispense?

That depends on the scale, valve, target quantity and product. Depending on the version, Füll dispensing centres achieve a dispensing accuracy of up to 0.1 g.

Can an existing dispensing system be extended?

Yes. Thanks to the modular design, additional valves, pipes and storage containers can be retrofitted. A retrofit of older systems is also possible.

How long does a system project take?

That depends on the scope. A single system is implemented much faster than a complete project with a tank farm; the Sherwin-Williams project with a tank farm and several systems took around 18 months.
